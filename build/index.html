01 How I work

  1. I learn a process fast.At the parts dealership, the first demo and the first real use came within one week.
  2. I look for where the time goes.The dealership's owner estimates that about 95% of orders come in by phone. So I worked on the phone call, not on another form.
  3. I change the plan when the facts change.My first design had a general LLM read the call after the caller hung up. Then the goal changed: the quote should fill in during the call, and the draft is decided again every few seconds. So I switched to a decision model that picks from fixed lists. In a test both were equally accurate, so speed decided it: the general LLM took 1.5 s per answer, the decision model 0.43 s.
  4. I check my own numbers.A list of top parts per car model looked like it covered 71.9% of order lines in the dealership's sales data (not public). When I used the customer's actual car instead of the car in the parts data, it was 57.3%. That is the number I report. Since then I use only what is known at the time of the call, and test on a later period than the one I built from.
  5. I keep the tools people already know.The office staff have used the same ERP for decades, and a very different tool would feel strange to them. So the new system stays close to the old one and only removes the steps they did by hand. To see how they really work in it, I log a working day: catalogue screens, ERP requests, calls and prints, in one timeline. The staff know about the logging.
  6. People make the final call.The bot cannot print, delete a document or give the final confirmation; a person does that. Small choices the staff asked me to stop asking them about, like which source a part comes from, the bot makes on its own.

Quote automation for a parts dealership

08–12/2026 · service contract
GitHub ↗

The dealership runs on an old ERP made for Internet Explorer. I built a web app for taking orders and a bot that types confirmed quotes into the ERP, reads them back, and stops for a person if something looks wrong. Printing and deleting a document are not on the bot's list of allowed actions, so it cannot do them. No language model runs in production. I work on it remotely from Munich, and development ends in 12/2026.

What I learned. Logs show what people do, not whether they are happy with it or what they actually want. The bot used to stop when the ERP listed one part under two sources. My rule mostly matched what the staff had picked in the logs, but they did not want to be asked every time. Now the bot always picks: a source with stock first, and parts with no stock are noted in the quote memo. The staff send suggestions like this through a box on the site, and I work through them one by one. Building a first version and changing it from their suggestions can be faster than trying to work everything out from the data.

Production code is private (customer data). The design is public, shown on made-up systems, including how a working day is logged ↗.

Live call-to-quote autofill

2026 · personal project, follow-up to the dealership system
GitHub ↗

A prototype: while a repair shop is on the phone, the parts quote fills itself in. The caller says “a fender for the Vera (a car model), left… no, the right one”, and the form follows. A decision model picks from fixed lists (car, body part, side, damage). It says how sure it is, and unsure fields stay empty for the person. Part numbers come from the catalogue, not from the model.

It builds on five years of the dealership's sales (not public; the repository runs on made-up data). I grouped the invoices into accidents (one insurance claim, plus any second claim for the same car within 30 days). 49% of accidents had more than one invoice, and 20% of part lines came on a later one. So the tool treats one accident as the whole repair, not one invoice. On 19 recent accidents, the dealership's quote tool, which looks part numbers up in the catalogue, matched 97% of the sold lines exactly (81% from sales history alone). The damaged zone and side were taken from the sale, so this checks the lookup, not the person's choice.

22 test calls (18 written scripts, 4 synthetic recordings, English only), 3 runs: 74–75 of 78 values filled, 0 wrong (general LLM: 77 filled, 0 wrong). 22 calls, most of them written by me, are too few to put a tight number on the error rate. Not tested yet: real phone audio, other languages.

Case checker for consumer advice

2026 · Legal Loves Tech hackathon, challenge by Verbraucherzentrale Bayern, developer in a team of 3
GitHub ↗

Consumer advisers check contracts and letters by hand, and the result can depend on who is on duty. Our tool reads the same documents and gives a verdict in the categories VZ Bayern asked for, with a template letter and a quote from the document for each reason. If a document is missing, it says which one. A fixed rule engine makes the decision, not a language model, and names stay on the computer.

The work continued after the hackathon: follow-up presentation to VZ Bayern, 10/2026.

LLMs to analyse business models

2025 · Bachelor's thesis, grade 1.3

A command-line tool where a local LLM (Llama 3.2 via Ollama, LangChain) reads UML and BPMN 2.0 / ARIS models stored as XML and answers questions about them. Large models are split into parts (Map-Collapse-Reduce), and edits asked in plain language are written back as valid XML.

Verifiable legal RAG

2026 · practice project before the Legal Loves Tech hackathon
GitHub ↗

Every claim the model makes must come with a word-for-word quote, and simple code checks that the quote is really in the source. If it isn't, the claim is marked as failed. Near matches (90% similar or more) also pass, with a separate mark, but only if every number and every “not” is the same as in the source. No frameworks, so every step can be inspected.
